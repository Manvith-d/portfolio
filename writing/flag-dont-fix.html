Flag, don’t fix: what bank reconciliation taught me about automation

October 21, 2026

In 2026 I spent nine months as an Automation Developer co-op on the SOEA Automation Development Team at Citizens Bank. The two automations I worked on most, CMOD Reject Reporting and Private Banking QC, are projected to save more than 1,000 hours a year between them.

Neither of them makes a final call on its own. That was deliberate, and it’s the most useful design lesson I took away from the co-op.

The job is reconciliation, not judgment

CMOD Reject Reporting reads daily reports, reconciles rejected-item counts against a shared workbook, and flags any mismatch for a person to review. Counting and comparing is exactly what software is good at: it’s repetitive, it has a right answer, and it’s easy to get wrong when a tired human does it every morning.

Deciding why two numbers disagree is different. It needs context the bot doesn’t have. So the automation does the part that has a right answer and hands over a short, precise list for the part that doesn’t. When the counts match, nobody needs to look. When they don’t, someone knows exactly where to start.

Treat the exception path as the main path

Most of the build used UiPath’s REFramework, which separates business exceptions (the data is valid but breaks a rule) from system exceptions (something technical failed and might work on retry). That split changed how I thought about mismatches. A mismatch isn’t the bot failing; it’s the bot doing its job. It deserves the same care in logging, reporting and testing as the happy path.

Ship a review artifact, not a verdict

Private Banking QC runs weekly. It consolidates report and API data, validates activity, and produces a structured Excel workbook that people review and archive. The workbook is the product. It has to be laid out so a reviewer can scan it quickly, and it has to be kept, because in banking a clear record of what was checked matters as much as the check itself.

Hypercare is where trust is earned

I supported both production deployment and hypercare, the period right after go-live when the team watches an automation closely. A bot that ran perfectly in testing still has to prove itself on real data, with real people relying on its output. Hypercare is when an automation stops being a project and becomes something the business trusts.

Write it down before you build it

One of my projects never became code at all: a solution design for checking screenshot-storage configuration across automation projects and reporting missing or inconsistent settings. Writing the design first made the scope, the checks and the remediation path explicit before anyone spent time building.

Automate the counting. Flag the exceptions. Leave the judgment to people.

That’s the rule I’d give anyone starting out in automation. It makes the bot simpler, makes the people around it more confident, and it’s why those 1,000 projected hours are hours that people actually get back.
