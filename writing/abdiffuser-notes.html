Diffusion beyond images: notes from reimplementing AbDiffuser

October 21, 2026 · 2 min read

Most people meet diffusion models through image generators: start with noise, and step by step, denoise it into a picture. AbDiffuser applies the same idea to something far less forgiving: antibodies.

With Pradyumna Raghavendra and Udaykumar Patel, I worked on a research implementation based on the AbDiffuser paper. Here’s what makes the problem interesting, and what implementing it taught me.

Two kinds of data, two kinds of noise

An antibody has a sequence (a string of amino acids, each one of 20 types) and a structure (the 3D coordinates of its atoms). These are different kinds of data, so they need different kinds of diffusion:

  • Coordinates are continuous, so they use Gaussian diffusion: add Gaussian noise, then learn to remove it.
  • Amino acids are categorical, so they use discrete diffusion: gradually corrupt the categories, then learn to restore them.

The hard part is doing both at once and keeping them consistent. A sequence that doesn’t match its generated structure isn’t a useful antibody.

A backbone built for antibodies

The model uses APMixer, the backbone proposed for this problem. Instead of treating a protein as an arbitrary graph, it takes advantage of the fact that antibodies share a common layout, mixing information along residue positions and across features.

Evaluating what you can’t eyeball

With images, you can look at the output. With antibodies, you can’t. We evaluated generated samples for sequence and structural diversity. Diversity matters because a model that keeps producing the same plausible antibody is useless for design. Diversity on its own isn’t enough, though: samples also have to be realistic, which is why evaluation needs more than one metric.

What reimplementing a paper teaches you

Reading a paper tells you what works. Implementing it shows you all the decisions the paper doesn’t spell out. Turning equations into working PyTorch and CUDA code, with a team, was the best way I’ve found to really understand a method.
